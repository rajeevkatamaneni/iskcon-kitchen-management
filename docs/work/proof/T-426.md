## The rule I chose, and what settled it

**The group's id, not the first position.** `applyMenuLayout` places a destination the arrangement
does not mention with

```ts
const home = mergedById.get(group.id);
if (home) home.items.push(item);
else newcomers.push(item);          // → the group headed "New", at the bottom
```

where `group` is the **standard** group the destination comes from and `mergedById` is keyed on the
ids in the temple's arrangement. So the question the merge asks is "is this standard group's *id*
still in the arrangement", and it never looks at where that group sits or what it is now called.
A first-position rule would therefore protect the wrong group the moment an administrator moves
another one to the top: the nine off-board destinations would still be looking for `main`, and
`main` — now second and unprotected — could be deleted out from under them.

**Worked out from `nav.ts` rather than written down as `"main"`.** The rule in words is "a group
that is the standard home of a destination this board never shows", and that is exactly the
expression:

```ts
const PROTECTED_GROUP_IDS: ReadonlySet<string> = new Set(
  standardMenu()
    .filter((group) => group.items.some((item) => !item.roles.includes("TEMPLE_ADMIN")))
    .map((group) => group.id)
);
```

Today that yields exactly one group — `main`, the unheaded first one, which holds the volunteer's
four and the operator's five. If a later release puts a volunteer-only destination into another
group, that group is protected on the day it happens. `nav.ts` could not be edited to export the id,
and hard-coding `"main"` here would have been a second copy of a fact that lives there.

**Precedence, because the two refusals overlap.** One group left, and it is the protected one, makes
both true. The **last group left wins**: with one group on the board that refusal binds whatever
group it is, and the protected one appears the moment a second group exists and it is the refusal
actually in the way. That also keeps T-422's existing test passing unchanged. One sentence is
rendered, never two — asserted in three of the five new tests.
