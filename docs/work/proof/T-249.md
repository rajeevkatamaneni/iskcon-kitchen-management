## API for the three callers

```java
IngredientNameMatcher.split(String raw) -> Split(String base, String preparation /* null if none */)
IngredientNameMatcher.normalise(String name) -> String
IngredientNameMatcher.findMatch(String candidate, Collection<Entry> existing) -> Optional<Match>
record Entry(UUID id, String name, List<String> aliases)      // also Entry(id, name)
record Match(Entry entry, MatchKind kind, String matchedName) // matchedName = the name or alias that matched
enum MatchKind { EXACT, CLOSE }
```

## The parsing rule (split), R-DUP-1

Preparation phrases are matched as whole phrases, case-insensitively:
chopped, chopped fine, grated, grated and squeezed, fresh grated, cubed, diced, diced small, crushed,
ground, coarsely ground, roasted and crushed, roasted and ground, roasted and skinned, soaked, soaked
overnight, sliced, shredded, crumbled, cut, cut and fried, boiled and cubed, paste, halved, slit, whisked,
fresh, sour, chilled, boiling.

Store-bought exception, never split: "Black pepper, ground" (it is held as the normalised key
`black pepper ground`, so "Black pepper ground" is covered too).

The rule runs in this order:
1. A store-bought exception comes back whole.
2. With commas:
   - An empty segment comes back whole. That covers a doubled comma, a trailing comma, or nothing
     before the first comma, and follows IngredientNames.
   - A tail opening with if, with, in, on, from, per, at, by, about or without comes back whole.
     Examples: "Urad dal, with skin" and "Potato, in fingers".
   - Otherwise tails are taken off **from the end** while each one is a preparation phrase or a
     purpose (opening with "for", "to" or "as"). The first tail that is neither stops the process
     and stays in the name.
3. A preparation phrase at the **front** of the head comes off, repeatedly, as long as an ingredient is left.
4. With no commas, a preparation phrase at the **end** also comes off. This covers "Curd sour" and "Ginger paste".
5. Brackets stay with the head. The note keeps the order the words were written in, front first, joined with ", ".

## The normalising rule, R-DUP-2

`normalise(name)` works in three steps:
1. It takes `split(name).base()`, which removes preparations and purposes as above.
2. It turns the text to lower case, drops accents ("purée" becomes puree), and turns every run of
   characters that are not letters or digits into one space. Brackets and hyphens go this way.
3. It makes each word singular:
   - words of 3 letters or fewer are unchanged
   - leaves → leaf
   - -llies → -lli (chillies → chilli); other -ies → -y (berries → berry)
   - -oes → -o (tomatoes → tomato)
   - -ches, -shes, -sses, -xes, -zes → drop -es
   - words ending -ss, -us or -is are unchanged (hibiscus)
   - any other word ending in -s drops the s (tomatos → tomato, cloves → clove)

## The matching rule, findMatch

The candidate is checked against each entry's name and every alias:

- **EXACT**: the normalised names are equal.
- **CLOSE**, first kind (a typing slip):
  - the same letters with the spaces in different places, or
  - the same number of words, exactly one word different, that word one edit away (a letter
    changed, added, dropped, or two neighbours swapped), and the longer version of that word at
    least 6 letters long.
- **CLOSE**, second kind (bare against qualified): one name has no comma qualifier after the split,
  and it equals the other's part before the first comma once both are normalised. Both names having
  qualifiers does not count, so two varieties never match each other.

The best match is chosen by rank (EXACT, then slip, then bare against qualified). A tie goes to the
shortest entry name, and after that to the order the entries were given.

**Why "Tomatos" meets "Tomato, ripe" as CLOSE, not EXACT.** The clarifier said "ripe" is not settled
as a preparation. So "ripe" stays in the name, and the AC is met by the bare-against-qualified rule.

**Why the limits are one edit, one word and six letters.**
- Two edits would make "ragi flour" (finger millet) close to "rava flour" (semolina).
- Short Indian ingredient names sit one letter apart and mean different food: Chana/Chena,
  Ghee/Gheer, Rava/Ragi.

**Measured on the real library** (`backend/src/main/resources/recipe-library/*.json`, 33 files):
- 2,238 distinct names become 1,849 normalised keys. 414 names get a preparation split off.
- 19 key pairs are close by spelling. 18 of them are the same thing spelled two ways: tulasi/tulsi
  leaf, panch phoran/phoron, sweet corn/sweetcorn, horse gram/horsegram, chole/chhole masala, and so on.
- 1 pair is wrong: **chana ~ chhana** (chickpea and chhena cheese).

Library qualifier counts: 984 of the 2,238 distinct names have a comma, and they have 382 distinct
tails. The biggest by line count are grated 987, chopped 471, roasted 391, crushed 343, ground 287,
cubed 201, split 160, diced 156, coarse 144, slit 116, "for frying" 113, thick 112 and ripe 103.
