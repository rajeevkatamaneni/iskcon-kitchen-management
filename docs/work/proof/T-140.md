# T-140 — the shopping list sums the stock ledger once per page load, not three times

**The headline, and it corrects the brief.** One `GET /api/v1/shopping-list` used to send
**three** statements against `stock_movements`, not nine. It now sends **one**. At T-139's
five-year scale — 146,150 movements — the page went from a median of **445 ms to 126 ms**, measured
on the same laptop twenty minutes apart.

**The nine was a measurement artefact, and finding that out was most of the work.** T-139 read the
count out of `pg_stat_all_tables` either side of one request. PostgreSQL flushes a backend's
counters when a transaction ends and at most about once a second, so after ten page loads at 430 ms
each that backend is permanently a couple of requests behind — and the requests still in flight when
the "before" snapshot is taken land inside the window and read as that one request's work. Three
statements, three loads' worth of backlog, nine. The same arithmetic explains every other row of
that table: `meal_plans` 9 (really 3), `ingredients` 4,185 (really ~1,443).

Both numbers below are now measured **twice, by instruments on opposite sides of the connection**,
and they agree exactly:

| | statements sent (JDBC) | readings of the whole ledger per load (`pg_stat`) | median GET |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Before** (fix reverted, same run as the control) | **3** | **3.00** | **445.1 ms** |
| **After** | **1** | **1.00** | **125.7 ms** |

Those are exactly the three the brief could see in the code. The other six were the statistics
counting the same three again.

**How that was established, since "the brief is wrong" needs better evidence than "I looked".**
A new test-side class, `StatementRecorder`, puts a recording `DataSource` in front of the
application's — the same seam, by the same mechanism, that `TenancyConfiguration` already uses to
apply the tenant to every connection, so nothing can issue a statement past it. It records the SQL
and the `org.iskcon.kms` stack frames at the moment of `prepareStatement`. It is off unless a test
switches it on, and it lives in the configuration the two perf classes already share so it forks no
Spring context.

Two things it removes that `pg_stat_all_tables` cannot:

- **The lag**, above.
- **The unit.** `idx_scan` counts *plan executions*, not statements. `purchase_order_lines` reads 26
  scans per page load from one statement, because its plan drives a nested loop over 26 purchase
  orders. A count of scans simply cannot answer *how many times did the application ask*, which is
  the question T-140 was set.

The `pg_stat` reading is kept in the harness rather than dropped — two instruments either side of
the connection is better than one — but it now brackets **twenty** page loads instead of one, so a
couple of requests in flight is a rounding error rather than a multiplier, and the report prints the
figure that matters as a division of two measured numbers: *readings of the whole ledger per page
load*. It comes out at exactly 1.00 with the fix and exactly 3.00 without it.

## Step 2 — what changed

The suggestions method already read the ledger as its first step. That map is now handed to the two
calls that used to read it again. **Both existing signatures are untouched**, so the four other
callers — the low-stock digest, the inventory screen's low list, the planner's sufficiency badge,
the `/inventory/items/low` endpoint — go on working with nothing changed.

- **`SufficiencyService.shortfallFeed(Map<UUID,BigDecimal>)`** — new. The no-argument form now
  delegates to it, reading the ledger itself. `allocateAcrossWindow` takes the map as a parameter.
- **`InventoryItemService.lowStock(Map<UUID,BigDecimal>)`** — new, returning a new nested record
  `LowStockLine(ingredientId, onHand, reorderThreshold)`.
- **`ShoppingListService.suggestions()`** — passes `onHandBase` to both.

**Two decisions inside that are worth reading, because both are places it could have gone quietly
wrong.**

**The allocation walk mutates the map it is given, and now takes a copy.** That is how a meal is
charged for the ones ahead of it — `remaining.put(ing, availBase.subtract(needBase))`. Handing it
the shopping list's own map would have left every line on the screen printing, as *current stock*,
whatever was left after a fortnight of planned meals had taken their share. One line —
`new LinkedHashMap<>(onHandBaseByIngredient)` — and a comment saying why it is there.

**`lowStock(Map)` returns a different type, on purpose.** The existing `lowStock()` gets its on-hand
figure from a *per-batch* aggregate, because the stock screen shows which lot expires first. Skipping
that read is the whole saving, and it means there is nothing left to say about expiry — so returning
a `StockItemView` would have handed back a row claiming `expiringSoon = false` and no soonest date
for every consumable in the temple. `LowStockLine` carries only the three facts the read actually
established. The two sums are arithmetically identical (a per-batch total summed across an
ingredient's batches *is* that ingredient's total), which is what makes the substitution exact rather
than approximately right; the *Low* rule itself was extracted into one `isBelowThreshold` that both
methods call, so the badge on the screen and the line on the list cannot drift apart.

**No cache, and the brief's reason stands.** Nothing is memoised and nothing has a lifetime; the
value is read once per request and passed down the stack. **The parameter reaches two call sites, not
five**, so the threshold the brief set for stopping and asking was never approached.

**And the correctness half, which is the part worth having.** The three reads sat inside one
`@Transactional(readOnly = true)`, and that buys less than it reads like: PostgreSQL at READ
COMMITTED gives each *statement* its own snapshot. A delivery recorded while the page was loading
could therefore be counted by one sum and missed by the others — the shortfall computed against one
figure for the rice, a different figure for the rice printed in the *current stock* column beside it,
on a screen somebody orders food from, with nothing on it to say anything had happened. One read
cannot do that. That reasoning is written into the code rather than only into this file.

## Acceptance criteria

**1 — find out where all the reads come from, and put the list in the proof.** Done, above, traced
at the JDBC boundary with caller chains rather than reasoned about. **The count is three, not nine**,
and the reason the brief said nine is stated with its mechanism. Verified by two independent
instruments agreeing in both directions (3 → 1 statements, 3.00 → 1.00 whole-ledger readings).

**2 — pass the answer in; do not cache it.** One map, read once in `suggestions()`, handed to two

`LowStockDigestIT`, `InventoryStockIT` and `MealPlanIT` are in the 71 above.

**3 — prove it with the scan count, not a stopwatch.** The assertion added to
`ShoppingListPerformanceIT` is `statements against stock_movements == 1`. There is still no duration
assertion anywhere in the harness, and I did not add one. The timing figures are reported because
they corroborate; nothing gates on them.

**4 — a guard that survives.** A second assertion of the same fact now runs in the **normal suite**,
in `ShoppingListIT`, taking no measurable time on a two-ingredient fixture. That matters because the
perf harness is gated behind `KMS_PERF` and nobody runs it by accident: without this, the count could
go back to three and CI would stay green.

**5 — the 4,185 index scans on a 150-row table.** Looked at, diagnosed, **not fixed** — see below.

**6 — tenant isolation.** Nothing about tenancy changed: no new SQL, no new predicate, no
`tenant_id` anywhere in this diff. Every query involved is the one that was already there, still
scoped by RLS from the verified token. The perf fixture generates and reads as `kms_app` under RLS,

## The `ingredients` reads — reported, not fixed, and here is what they actually are

T-139 recorded 4,185 index scans against a 150-row table and guessed at a per-ingredient lookup in a
loop. **It is neither 4,185 nor a loop over ingredients.** Corrected for the lag it is ~1,443 index
scans per page load, and the statement counter names the source exactly:

```
  The ten methods that sent the most statements of any kind:
       180  recipe.RecipeService.get:100
       180  recipe.RecipeService.get:114
        14  auth.AuthenticationFilter.authenticate:126
```

**360 of the page's 404 statements are recipe reads.** `RecipeService.get` issues two — the head row,
and `recipe_ingredients JOIN ingredients WHERE recipe_id = ?`, whose nested loop is where the
`ingredients` scans come from, roughly eight per recipe. It is called once per distinct
*(recipe, target yield)* in the buying window, memoised on that pair — and **the whole thing happens
twice per page load**, because `CommittedStockService.claims()` runs once for the sufficiency walk
(`claimsInHorizon()`) and again for the low-stock read (`committedBaseByIngredient()`).

That is a genuinely different shape of problem from T-140's:

- It is **cheap per statement and expensive in round trips** — 2,026,160 rows read over 20 loads,
  but across 3,600 statements, none of which touches a large table. Behind Cloud Run and Cloud SQL,
  where each round trip carries network latency that a local container does not, this is likely to
  cost *more* than the ledger sum did.
- **Both files are outside this contract.** `RecipeService.java` is not in it, and neither is
  `CommittedStockService.java` — my contract grants `InventoryItemService.java` alone out of the
  inventory package. Fixing the duplicate `claims()` pass means changing what one of them returns.
- **It is a second parameter on the same path**, which is precisely the signal the brief said to stop
  at rather than improvise.

**What I would put in the next task's brief:** the plan's claims are computed twice per page load,
and each computation reads every distinct recipe-and-yield in the horizon with two statements. Fixing
the duplication halves it; fixing the N+1 in `RecipeService.get` — one query for the lines of many
recipes instead of one query per recipe — takes most of the rest. Worth measuring before assuming the
order: the harness now prints the per-method statement counts, so the next person starts where I
finished rather than where T-139 did.
